How Flow Lab works

The model ladder

Every prediction in engineering is a model, and each step up adds physics and cost. Flow Lab covers the first two steps and prepares files for the next two.

1 · Ideal quasi-1DIsentropic flow plus normal shocks. Instant. Predicts regimes, thresholds and trends exactly for a perfect gas.
2 · Realistic 1-DAdds boundary layers, discharge coefficient, divergence loss and separation. Instant. Typically within a few percent when the flow stays attached.
3 · CFD (ANSYS)Solves 2-D or 3-D Navier–Stokes. Hours. Captures curved shocks, separation and the jet. Use the Export tab.
4 · ExperimentWall pressure taps, mass flow and thrust. The final judge of every model above.

Ideal model (the first model)

The gas is calorically perfect (constant γ and R), the flow is steady and one-dimensional (properties vary only along the axis), and there is no friction or heat transfer. Away from shocks the flow is isentropic:

T₀/T = 1 + (γ−1)/2 · M²    P₀/P = (T₀/T)^(γ/(γ−1))    ρ = P/(R·T)    V = M·√(γRT)

Mass conservation links area and Mach number. For each area ratio there is one subsonic and one supersonic solution:

A/A* = (1/M) · [ (2/(γ+1)) · (1 + (γ−1)/2 · M²) ]^((γ+1)/(2(γ−1)))

When the throat reaches Mach 1 the nozzle is choked, and the mass flow can no longer change with back pressure:

ṁ = A_t · P₀ · √(γ/(R·T₀)) · (2/(γ+1))^((γ+1)/(2(γ−1)))

A normal shock is an abrupt jump from supersonic to subsonic flow. Total temperature is conserved but total pressure drops:

M₂² = (1 + (γ−1)/2·M₁²) / (γM₁² − (γ−1)/2)    P₂/P₁ = 1 + 2γ/(γ+1)·(M₁² − 1)

How the regime is found. Three back pressures divide a converging–diverging nozzle's behaviour. Above the choking pressure the flow is subsonic everywhere. Between the choking pressure and the shock-at-exit pressure a normal shock stands inside the nozzle; Flow Lab finds its position by bisection so that the exit pressure equals Pb. Below the shock-at-exit pressure the flow inside is fully supersonic and the jet adjusts outside: overexpanded above the ideal exit pressure, underexpanded below it.

Realistic model

Each correction is a standard engineering estimate. Together they usually bring the 1-D prediction within a few percent of measurements for attached flow. They are estimates, so the Compare tab always shows them next to the ideal model.

Boundary layer. Friction slows a thin layer of gas at the wall. Its displacement thickness δ* acts like a smaller nozzle. Flow Lab grows δ* along the wall with the turbulent flat-plate law, using properties at the Eckert reference temperature for compressibility (laminar Blasius law below Re = 5×10⁵):

δ* ≈ 0.046 · s / Re_s^0.2    T_ref = T + 0.5(T_w − T) + 0.22(T_r − T), T_w = T_r (adiabatic wall)

The effective area π(r − δ*)² lowers the throat area (mass flow) and the effective area ratio (exit Mach). Accuracy is about ±30% on δ* itself, which is usually a 1–3% effect on the results.

Throat curvature. A curved sonic line passes less flow than a flat one. Leading term of Hall's (1962) analysis, with R = Rc/Rt floored at 1:

C_d,inviscid ≈ 1 − (γ+1) / (96 · R²)    C_d = C_d,inviscid × C_d,boundary-layer

Divergence loss. Gas leaving a cone at half-angle α does not all point backwards, so only part of the momentum becomes thrust: λ = (1 + cos α)/2. A 15° cone loses 1.7%. Flow Lab uses the exit wall angle θe for bells, which is an approximation.

Flow separation. In a strongly overexpanded nozzle the wall pressure falls so far below ambient that the boundary layer cannot push against the rise, and the jet peels off the wall. Flow Lab marches along the wall and flags the first point that meets the selected criterion:

Summerfield: P_w < 0.4·P_b    Schmucker: P_w/P_b < (1.88·M_w − 1)^(−0.64)

Downstream of separation the wall pressure is drawn rising to Pb, and thrust is computed at the separation point. That is why a separated nozzle can produce more thrust than the ideal model predicts for full overexpanded flow. Real separation is unsteady and creates side loads, so design to avoid it.

What the realistic model still leaves out: friction losses of total pressure, heat transfer, 2-D throat flow, oblique and curved shocks, shock–boundary-layer interaction details, real-gas effects and condensation. CFD and experiments cover these.

Nozzle shapes

Conical nozzles are easy to make and analyse. The diverging half-angle trades length (and friction) against divergence loss; 12–15° is the usual choice. A rounded throat (Rc/Rt of 1–2) gives a cleaner sonic line than a sharp corner.

Bell nozzles (Rao's thrust-optimised parabola) turn the flow quickly just after the throat, then straighten it towards the exit. An 80% bell is 20% shorter than a 15° cone with about the same loss. Flow Lab builds it with the standard construction: an upstream throat arc of 1.5·Rt, a downstream arc of 0.382·Rt ending at angle θn, then a parabola (quadratic Bézier) to the exit at angle θe. The automatic angles come from Rao's 80% chart (approximate). For other lengths, look up the angles and enter them yourself.

Converging nozzles can only reach Mach 1 at the exit. They are the simplest way to see choking.

Troubleshooting

My exit Mach number is lower than the target.

Check the regime first: an internal shock or unchoked flow means the target cannot be reached at this back pressure (see Check & fix). If the flow is shock-free, the boundary layer has reduced the effective area ratio. Compare tab shows by how much. Enlarge the exit slightly or accept the lower Mach.

Mass flow does not change when I lower the back pressure.

That is choking, and it is correct. Once the throat is sonic, ṁ depends only on P₀, T₀, the gas and the throat area. Raise P₀ or enlarge the throat to get more flow.

There is a shock inside my nozzle.

The back pressure is too high for this area ratio. Raise P₀ above the value Check & fix suggests, lower Pb, or reduce the exit diameter. Every internal shock wastes total pressure.

Measured mass flow is lower than predicted.

Expect Cd of about 0.95–0.99. Measure the as-built throat diameter; a 0.1 mm error on a 10 mm throat is a 2% flow error. Also check that P₀ is measured in a low-speed plenum (contraction ratio above 3), not in a fast-moving pipe.

Wall pressures near the exit read higher than predicted.

The flow has probably separated. Compare with the realistic model's separation point, raise P₀ or shorten the nozzle, and look for unsteady readings.

There is fog or frost in the jet.

The static temperature at high Mach can drop below the frost point of moisture in shop air, or even near air's own condensation point. Use dry air or bottled nitrogen, or heat the supply.

My ANSYS Fluent run does not converge.

Use the density-based solver, start at a low CFL (1–5), initialise with the 1-D inlet pressure from the setup sheet, and refine the mesh near the throat and walls. Strongly overexpanded cases with separation are unsteady by nature; try a lower back pressure first.

The STL import in NX looks faceted.

STL stores triangles. Increase the facet count in Export, or import the half-section DXF and use Revolve for an exact, editable solid.

The printed nozzle leaked or cracked.

Printed plastics are weak between layers and soften with heat. Check the wall-strength item in Check & fix, print with 100% infill, and proof-test with water at 1.5× the operating pressure before using gas.
